The pushdown is the easiest way to put direct work on your triceps. You stand still, the cable does the stabilizing, and the only joint that moves is your elbow, so almost all of the effort ends up in the back of your arm. That makes it a good pick after bench or overhead pressing, when your shoulders are tired but your triceps still have work left in them, and a sensible first triceps exercise for anyone new to the gym.

## How to do a tricep pushdown

1. **Set the pulley at the top and clip on a rope or short bar.** Stand facing the stack, close enough that the cable hangs almost straight down in front of you, usually a foot or so away.
2. **Grab the handle and pull it down to chest height.** With a bar, use an overhand grip about 6 inches (15 cm) apart. With a rope, hold it just below the knobs with your thumbs pointing up.
3. **Pin your elbows to your sides.** Your upper arms should hang close to vertical with your elbows touching or nearly touching your ribs. A slight forward lean from the hips is fine; keep your chest up and your shoulders down away from your ears.
4. **Push the handle down until your arms are straight.** Stop when your elbows are fully locked and the handle is at the front of your thighs. With a rope, pull the ends apart at the bottom so your hands finish beside your hips.
5. **Squeeze for a second at the bottom.** You should feel the back of your upper arm cramp slightly. If you feel it in your chest or shoulders instead, your upper arms are moving.
6. **Let the handle rise until your forearms pass parallel to the floor.** Your hands end up around the bottom of your chest, with your elbows still in the same spot by your sides. Take one to two seconds on the way up, then start the next rep.

The only thing that should change during a rep is the angle at your elbow. If your shoulders, hips or upper arms are moving, the stack is doing less for your triceps than it looks.

## Cues that help

**"Elbows glued to your ribs."** Picture a strip of tape holding each elbow to your side. It's the fix for upper arms that drift forward on the way up, which turns the move into a half press.

**"Push the handle through the floor."** Aim past the bottom of the rep instead of stopping when your arms look straight. It gets you the full lockout, which is where the triceps work hardest.

**"Snap the rope apart."** On the rope version, think about pulling the ends away from each other as you finish. If that doesn't click, try "hands to your pockets". Either way you'll get a harder squeeze at the bottom.

**"Shoulders in your back pockets."** Keep your shoulders pulled down and back. When they roll forward and up, your chest and front delts join in and the triceps get less of the load.

## Common mistakes

**Elbows drifting out or forward.** As the set gets hard, the elbows flare out to the sides or float up in front of you, and the pushdown turns into a sort of downward press. The usual cause is a weight that's slightly too heavy. Drop a plate on the stack and keep the elbows touching your sides for every rep.

**Leaning over the handle.** Some lean is normal, but if you're folding forward and throwing your bodyweight onto the handle, your triceps aren't the thing moving the stack. It looks like someone pushing a stuck door shut. Stand taller, soften your knees and take some weight off until you can finish the set without using your body.

**Stopping short of lockout.** Reps that end with a slight bend in the elbow let you move more weight, but they skip the part of the range where the triceps are fully shortened and working hardest. Straighten your arms completely on every rep, even if the weight has to come down.

**Letting the handle fly up.** If the stack yanks your hands up to your chin between reps, you lose control of your elbows and give up the lowering half of the rep. Stop the handle around chest height every time.

## Sets, reps and how much weight to use

Pushdowns suit moderate to high reps. For muscle growth, 3 sets of 10 to 15 is a good default, with the last rep or two of each set feeling hard. Pick a weight you could do two or three more clean reps with. Going lower than about 8 reps doesn't add much here, because the heavier the stack gets, the harder it is to keep your body out of it.

Do them after your main presses, not before. Tired triceps make your bench and overhead press weaker, and those lifts matter more. If you already press a couple of times a week, 6 to 10 direct triceps sets a week on top is plenty for most people.

Don't compare numbers between gyms. Cable machines use different pulley setups, so "40 on the stack" can be twice as heavy on one machine as another. A common starting point is somewhere around 20 to 40 lb (10 to 20 kg) on a standard stack, then adjust from there.

To progress, add reps before weight. Once you hit 15 clean reps on every set, move the pin down one plate and work back up from 10. The jumps on a cable stack are large relative to the weight you're using, so this matters more than it does on a barbell lift ([progressive overload](/blog/progressive-overload) covers the idea in more detail). ## Rope, bar or V-bar?

We'd start most people on the rope. It lets your wrists turn to whatever angle feels natural, and spreading the ends at the bottom gives you a fuller lockout. It's also the kindest option if your elbows get achy.

A straight bar or V-bar lets you load more, because both hands push one solid handle. That's useful once you're past the beginner stage and want a heavier option. Honestly, the difference between attachments matters less than people think; pick the one that feels best on your elbows and stick with it long enough to get stronger.

## Variations and alternatives

- **Reverse-grip pushdown:** an underhand grip on a straight bar. It's harder to load and feels different through the forearms. Some people find it easier on the elbows.
- **Single-arm pushdown:** one hand on a cable handle. Good for evening out a weaker arm, and it makes it obvious when one elbow is drifting.
- **Band pushdown:** loop a resistance band over a door frame or pull-up bar. It's the swap for training at home without a cable.
- **Overhead cable extension:** face away from the stack and extend your arms overhead. It stretches the long head of the triceps, which pushdowns don't do much.
- **[Skull crusher](/exercises/skull-crusher):** the free-weight option. Harder on the elbows, but it trains the triceps in a more stretched position.
- **[Tricep dip](/exercises/tricep-dip):** a bodyweight compound move that loads the triceps heavily along with your chest and shoulders.

## Tricep pushdown questions

### Are pushdowns enough to grow your triceps?

They'll get you a long way, especially if you also bench and overhead press. For the long head, add an exercise with your arms overhead. A 12-week study by Maeo and colleagues, published in 2023, had untrained adults train one arm with overhead cable extensions and the other with pushdowns, and the overhead arm grew noticeably more triceps overall. It was a small group of beginners, so we wouldn't drop pushdowns over it, but doing both is a sensible default.

### Why do I feel pushdowns in my elbows?

Usually it's too much weight, fast lowering or a straight bar forcing your wrists into an angle they don't like. Switch to the rope, lighten the load and slow the way up. If the ache is still there after a couple of weeks of lighter work, get it looked at.

### Should I lean forward or stand straight?

A small forward lean, maybe 10 to 20 degrees from the hips, is fine and lets the cable line up with your arms. The problem is lean that changes during the set. Pick your angle before the first rep and hold it.
