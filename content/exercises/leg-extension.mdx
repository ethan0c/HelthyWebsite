The leg extension is the only common exercise that trains your quads with nothing else joining in. No balance, no bracing, no glutes or lower back. That makes it a useful finisher after squats or presses, when your quads still have work left in them but you're too tired to do another heavy compound lift safely. It's also a good way to train your legs when your back or hips need a rest. Don't expect it to replace a squat, though; it's a supplement, not a foundation.

## How to do a leg extension

1. **Line your knees up with the machine's pivot.** Adjust the backrest so the backs of your knees sit at the front edge of the seat and your knee joint is level with the axle the arm rotates around. Most machines have a colored dot or marker on the pivot to help.
2. **Set the pad on your lower shins.** It should sit just above your ankles, on the front of your shin, not on your feet or halfway up your leg.
3. **Hold the handles and sit back.** Keep your hips down in the seat and your back against the pad.
4. **Straighten your legs until your knees are fully extended.** Squeeze your quads hard at the top for about a second. You should see the muscle above your kneecap tighten.
5. **Lower over 2 to 3 seconds.** Stop when your knees are back to about 90 degrees or a little more, just before the weight stack touches down, then start the next rep.

You should feel it along the front of your thighs, with a strong burn toward the end of the set. A burn in the quads is expected. A sharp or grinding pain in or under the kneecap isn't.

## Cues that help

**"Kick the pad toward the ceiling."** Gets you to finish every rep at full extension.

**"Squeeze and hold at the top."** A one-second pause fixes swinging and makes lighter weights work harder. If that doesn't land, try "show the muscle above your knee".

**"Hips pinned."** Pulling down on the handles stops your hips sliding forward to help the weight up.

## Common mistakes

**Swinging the weight up.** A fast kick from the bottom lets momentum do the work, and the pad often flies up and crashes back down. The quads barely work in the middle of the rep. Slow down, pause briefly at the top, and take 2 to 3 seconds to lower.

**Not fully extending.** Stopping with your knees still bent skips the top of the range, where the quads are fully shortened and the leg extension trains them in a way squats don't. If you can't reach full extension, the weight is too heavy.

**Using too heavy a weight.** It's easy to load more than your quads can handle on this machine, and it shows as half reps, hips lifting out of the seat and the stack slamming. The leg extension responds well to lighter weights for more reps, so there's no reason to grind out heavy sets. Pick a load that lets you pause at the top on every rep.

**Setting the seat wrong.** If your knees sit in front of or behind the pivot, the pad slides up and down your shin during each rep and puts odd stress on your knees. Adjust the backrest until the pad stays put.

## Is the leg extension bad for your knees?

It has that reputation, and for healthy knees it's mostly undeserved. Physical therapists use knee extensions in rehab constantly, often with a limited range. What makes it uncomfortable for some people is heavy weights, fast reps and a seat set so that the knee lines up badly with the pivot.

If your knees are sensitive, try a lighter weight for higher reps and limit the bottom of the range, stopping when your knees are bent to about 60 degrees rather than 90. If it still hurts after a couple of weeks of adjustments, get it looked at.

## Sets, reps and how much weight to use

The leg extension works best for higher reps: 2 to 4 sets of 10 to 20. Pick a weight you could do 1 or 2 more reps with, and it's a safe exercise to take to failure on your last set. Put it at the end of your leg session, after squats, presses or lunges. Rest 60 to 90 seconds.

## Variations and alternatives

- **Single-leg extension:** one leg at a time on the same machine. Useful for evening out a weaker side.
- **Reclined leg extension:** lean back slightly or recline the backrest if your machine allows. It puts more stretch on the rectus femoris, the quad muscle that also crosses the hip.
- **Resistance band leg extension:** tie a band behind you and extend against it while seated on a bench. A reasonable home option, if not as good as the machine.
- **Sissy squat:** a bodyweight exercise where you lean back and bend at the knees. Hard on the quads and needs no machine, but build up slowly.
- **[Leg press](/exercises/leg-press):** a compound swap if your gym has no extension machine. Put your feet low on the platform for more quad work.

## Leg extension questions

### Can leg extensions replace squats?

No. The leg extension only trains your quads, while squats also train your glutes, adductors and trunk and teach you to handle a heavy load on your feet. Use it alongside squats or presses, not instead of them.

### Should my toes point in or out?

Turning your toes slightly in or out shifts the work between parts of the quads a little, but the effect is small and not worth chasing. Keep them pointing straight ahead and your feet relaxed.
