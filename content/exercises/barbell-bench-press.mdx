The bench press is the lift most people judge their strength by, and it's worth doing well. It lets you load your chest, front delts and triceps heavier than any other upper-body press, and it's easy to track: the bar either went up or it didn't. What trips people up is the setup. Get that right and the press itself mostly takes care of itself.

## How to bench press

1. **Lie on the bench with your eyes under the bar.** Plant your feet flat on the floor, a bit wider than your hips and slightly behind your knees if your mobility allows it.
2. **Grip the bar just outside shoulder width.** For most people that puts the index fingers somewhere around the rings on the bar. Wrap your thumbs around it and set the bar low in your palm, over your forearm bones, not up by your fingers.
3. **Pull your shoulder blades back and down.** Squeeze them together as if you're trying to hold a pencil between them, then tuck them toward your back pockets. This gives you a small arch in your upper back and a stable shelf to press from.
4. **Unrack and settle.** Press the bar off the hooks and bring it forward until it's over your shoulders. Take a breath and hold it.
5. **Lower the bar to your lower chest.** It should touch around your nipple line or just below, with your elbows tucked roughly 45 to 75 degrees from your body. Take one to two seconds on the way down.
6. **Press back up and slightly back.** Drive the bar up toward your face so it finishes over your shoulders again, and breathe out once you're past the hardest part. The path is a gentle diagonal, not a straight line.

Your butt stays on the bench the whole time. The arch comes from your upper back, not from lifting your hips.

## Cues that help mid-set

**"Bend the bar."** Try to snap the bar in half by twisting your hands outward. You won't move the bar, but your lats and upper back switch on and your elbows fall into a safer angle without you thinking about it.

**"Push the floor away."** Your legs don't press the bar, but pushing through your feet keeps your whole body tight. If your feet slide around or come up on their toes, you'll feel the bar wobble.

**"Chest to the bar."** On the way down, think about meeting the bar with your chest rather than dropping the bar to you. It keeps your upper back arched and shortens the distance the bar travels.

If none of those click, try "squeeze the bar like you're wringing out a towel". Different cues land for different people, so keep the one that makes the rep feel smoother and forget the rest.

## Common mistakes

**Flaring your elbows out wide.** Elbows at 90 degrees to your body looks strong but puts your shoulders in a bad spot, and it's the usual cause of that pinchy feeling at the front of the shoulder. Tuck them to about 45 to 75 degrees. The "bend the bar" cue fixes this for most people.

**Bouncing the bar off your chest.** It feels like it helps you move more weight, and it does, for a bit. It also takes the load off the bottom of the lift, which is where you need to get stronger. Touch the chest, don't slam it.

**Lifting your hips.** When a rep gets hard, it's tempting to drive your hips up to turn the bench into a decline press. In a competition that's a failed lift, and in the gym it shifts stress onto your lower back. If it keeps happening, the weight is too heavy.

**Pressing straight up over your chest.** If the bar goes straight up from where it touched, it ends up over your stomach, a long way from your shoulders. It's like holding a heavy box out at arm's length instead of close to your body: same weight, much harder. Aim the bar back toward the rack as you press.

**Cutting the range short.** Stopping a few inches above your chest lets you load more, but you miss the part of the movement that builds the most strength. Take every rep to the chest unless you're deliberately doing a partial variation.

## Breathing and bracing

Take a big breath before you lower the bar and hold it on the way down. It stiffens your torso so the bar has a stable base to press from. Breathe out forcefully on the way up, once you're past the sticking point. On a set of 8 to 12 you can reset your breath at the top of each rep; on heavy sets of 3 to 5, most lifters take one breath and hold it for a rep or two.

## Sets, reps and how much weight to use

For strength, work in the 3 to 6 rep range for 3 to 5 sets. For muscle growth, sets of 6 to 12 work well, and 10 to 15 total hard sets of chest work a week is a good target. Pick a weight you could do two or three more reps with at the end of each set. Bench first in the session while you're fresh, and rest two to three minutes between heavy sets. It feels long, but you'll get more reps.

Warm up by ramping toward your working weight instead of jumping straight to it. If your working sets are 185 lb (85 kg), something like this works:

- Empty bar for 10 reps
- 95 lb (45 kg) for 5
- 135 lb (60 kg) for 3
- 165 lb (75 kg) for 1 or 2

Then start your working sets. Keep the warm-ups easy. They're practice, not training.

If you're new, start with the empty bar (45 lb or 20 kg) to learn the groove, then add 5 to 10 lb a session while every rep still looks the same. Once that stops working, switch to [adding a rep or two each week](/blog/progressive-overload) before you add weight.

## Variations and alternatives

- **[Dumbbell bench press](/exercises/dumbbell-bench-press):** easier on the shoulders because each hand finds its own path. Good if the barbell version bothers you, or as your second chest exercise.
- **[Incline bench press](/exercises/incline-barbell-bench-press):** puts more work on your upper chest and front delts. Most people use about 70 to 80% of their flat bench weight.
- **[Push-up](/exercises/push-up):** the same pattern with no equipment. Add a weighted vest or raise your feet once 20 reps is easy.
- **[Chest press machine](/exercises/chest-press-machine):** a fixed path and no need for a spotter, so it's handy for high-rep sets to failure at the end of a session.

## Bench press questions

### Do I need a spotter?

Not if you bench in a rack with safety bars set just below your chest at the bottom of the rep. If a rep doesn't go up, lower the bar onto the pins and slide out. Without safeties, get a spotter for anything close to your limit, and don't use collars so you can tip the plates off if you get stuck.

### Should my feet be flat or on my toes?

Either is fine as long as they stay planted and you can push through them. Flat feet are easier to learn. Some lifters get a tighter arch with their heels up, which is allowed in most powerlifting federations, but it's a refinement, not a requirement.

### Why do my shoulders hurt after benching?

Usually it's elbows flaring too wide, shoulder blades not pinned back, or too much volume too soon. Fix the setup and drop the weight for a couple of weeks. If it still hurts after that, get it looked at by a physical therapist rather than pushing through.

### How often should I bench?

Two or three times a week works well for most people, with one heavier day and one or two lighter ones. If you're tracking your sets, you can see exactly what you did last session and aim to beat it by a rep, which is how the number keeps going up.
