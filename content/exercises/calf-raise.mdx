Your calves get some work from squats, lunges and walking, but not enough to grow much. If you want bigger or stronger calves, or ankles that handle running and jumping better, you need to train them directly, and the calf raise is how. It's simple to learn and hard to do well. Most people rush through short, bouncy reps with too much weight, and that's the main reason calves have a reputation for being stubborn.

## How to do a calf raise

These steps are for a standing calf raise on a step, block or machine. The same rules apply to every version.

1. **Put the balls of your feet on the edge of the platform.** Your heels hang off the back, feet about hip width apart and pointing straight ahead. If you're on a machine, set the shoulder pads so your legs are straight when you stand under them.
2. **Stand tall with your knees straight but not locked.** Hold on to something for balance if you need to. The goal is to train your calves, not your balance.
3. **Lower your heels as far below the platform as they'll go.** Take 2 to 3 seconds. You should feel a strong stretch in your calves and up the back of your lower leg.
4. **Pause at the bottom for about a second.** No bounce.
5. **Rise up onto the balls of your feet as high as you can.** Push through your big toe and the ball of your foot until your heels are as high as they'll go.
6. **Pause at the top for a second, then lower slowly back to the stretch.** That's one rep.

You should feel it in the muscle of your calves, especially at the bottom and top of each rep. If you feel it mostly in your feet or the front of your shins, check that you're rising onto the ball of your foot, not your toes.

## Cues that help

**"Stand on your tiptoes as tall as you can."** Gets you to the top of the range instead of stopping halfway.

**"Pause in the stretch."** Stops the bounce that lets your Achilles tendon do the work. If that doesn't land, count "one-one-thousand" at the bottom before each rep.

**"Big toe down."** Keeps the pressure on the inside of your foot so your ankles don't roll out as you rise.

## Common mistakes

**Not using the full range.** Short reps in the middle of the movement are the most common calf raise fault by far. Your heels never drop below the platform and never get high at the top. The stretched bottom position is where the calves seem to get the most out of each rep, so you want every inch of it. Lighten the weight until you can go from a full stretch to fully up on your toes.

**Bouncing at the bottom.** Your Achilles tendon works like a spring. Drop into the bottom quickly and it stores energy and throws you back up, so your calves do less of the work. It feels easier because it is. A one-second pause at the bottom takes the spring out and makes the muscle do the lift.

**Bending your knees during the rep.** When a set gets hard, your knees start to bend and straighten to help drive the weight up. That turns it into a small squat and takes the work off your calves. Keep your knees in the same slightly soft position throughout, and if you can't, drop the weight.

**Loading more than you can control.** Calf machines make it easy to stack on weight, and the result is usually all three mistakes at once. Pick a load you can move through the full range with pauses.

## Straight knees or bent knees?

Your calf is two main muscles. The gastrocnemius is the bigger one you can see, and it crosses the knee, so it works hardest when your legs are straight. The soleus sits underneath and works whether your knees are bent or straight, so a seated calf raise with bent knees puts more of the work on it.

For most people we'd do standing raises as the main exercise and add seated raises if you want more calf work. Both muscles grow, and the soleus is bigger than it looks from the outside.

## Sets, reps and how much weight to use

Calves respond well to moderate and high reps: 3 to 4 sets of 10 to 20. Use a weight you could do 1 or 2 more reps with, while keeping the full range and the pauses. Rest about a minute between sets. Calves recover quickly, so training them two to four times a week works well. Do them at the end of a leg session or on any day you've got 10 minutes spare.

Start with bodyweight on a step. Once 3 sets of 20 with pauses feel easy, add a dumbbell in one hand, move to a machine, or switch to single-leg raises.

## Variations and alternatives

- **Single-leg calf raise:** one foot on a step, holding a dumbbell on the same side. Doubles the load without any equipment and shows up a weaker side.
- **Seated calf raise:** bent knees shift more work to the soleus. A good second calf exercise.
- **Leg press calf raise:** balls of your feet on the bottom edge of the leg press platform, pushing with your toes. Handy if your gym has no calf machine. Keep the safety catches in place.
- **Smith machine calf raise:** stand on a block with the bar on your back. Lets you load it heavily with good balance.

## Calf raise questions

### Why won't my calves grow?

Usually the reps are too short and too bouncy. Try a month of full-range reps with a pause at the bottom, 3 to 4 times a week, before you decide your calves are just stubborn. Genetics play a bigger part here than for most muscles, but most people are nowhere near their limit.

### Should I point my toes in or out?

Pointing your toes slightly in or out shifts the work a little between the inner and outer parts of the calf. Straight ahead is a sensible default.

### Are calf raises good for runners?

Yes. Strong calves help your ankles handle the repeated landings of running. Single-leg raises and seated raises are both good choices; build up the volume slowly if you're adding them on top of a running plan.
