A personal trainer charging $50–$100 a session costs $5,200–$10,400 a year at two sessions a week. An app subscription costs a fraction of that. So it's a fair question: is the cheaper option good enough for you?

While an AI coach can write and adjust a program and answer training and food questions from your logged numbers, it can't watch your squat and correct your form on the next rep, spot a heavy bench or notice a limp. If you already know the main lifts and can turn up on your own, an AI coach covers most of the things you'd pay a trainer for. If you're new to training, need an appointment to show up, or are working around an injury, pay for a trainer, at least for a while.

One admission up front: we make an AI coach, so weigh our view with that in mind. We've tried to be straight about its limitations.

## What you're actually paying a trainer for

A trainer has several jobs bundled together:

- **Programming** which exercises you do, how many sets and reps, and how often.
- **Adjusting** when you progress, stall, get sick or get busy.
- **Answering questions** about food, soreness, recovery and other questions you have.
- **Keeping you accountable** because you booked a slot and know someone is expecting you.
- **Coaching your movement** by watching, cueing, correcting, and spotting you.
- **Judgment about you in particular**, by noticing when you're tired, anxious or quietly avoiding the exercise that hurts.

A decent AI coach can cover the first three. The last three are where a good human trainer earns the extra money.

## Where a human trainer is clearly better

### Watching you move

A trainer can see your knees cave on the third rep of a squat, tell you to push them out, and watch the next rep to check if it worked. A text coach can't watch your reps. You can describe what you think is happening, but lifters don't always feel the faults a coach can see.

Learning a lift is a bit like learning to drive: you want an instructor in the passenger seat for the first few lessons, while the habits are forming, but you don't need one on every commute for the rest of your life.

If you're new to barbell lifts, we'd spend money on 3–5 sessions with a good coach in your first couple of months, focused on the [squat](/exercises/barbell-squat), [deadlift](/exercises/deadlift) and [bench press](/exercises/barbell-bench-press). Form coaching is most useful while you're learning the movements. Once you can do the lifts well, filming yourself from the side and comparing it with a good guide gets you a long way.

A trainer can also spot you on a heavy bench press. An app can't take the bar when a rep fails.

### Injuries, pain and medical conditions

If you're recovering from an injury, have a joint that hurts every time you train, are pregnant, or have a heart or other medical condition that affects exercise, start with your doctor or physical therapist. Then look for a trainer with experience in your situation. A good trainer's value here is judgment: knowing which movements to substitute and when to back off, based on what they can see and what you tell them. Software can suggest a swap, but it can't examine you.

### Showing up

Accountability is underrated. Knowing someone is waiting for you at 7am, and that you've paid for the hour, gets you out of bed in a way that a streak counter doesn't. Streaks and weekly check-ins work for some people. For others they're easy to ignore.

If you've joined and quit a gym several times, the booked appointment is probably the most valuable thing a trainer sells you, more than the program. In that case it's money well spent even if the workouts themselves seem ordinary. (A training partner who texts you when you skip training is a cheaper version of the same thing.)

### Reading the room

A good trainer notices things you don't say: that you're off today, that you flinch before overhead work, that you're sandbagging your sets. They adjust on the spot. An AI coach only knows what you log and what you tell it.

## What an AI coach can actually do

### Creating a program and adjusting it

An AI coach can build and adjust a program around your goal, schedule and equipment. Only have 45 minutes on Thursday? Moved to a gym without a squat rack? You can ask for a change when you need it, without waiting until your next session.

There's a caveat that applies to trainers too: most programming for most people isn't complicated. Getting stronger mostly comes down to training consistently and [progressive overload](/blog/progressive-overload), and a lot of what separates good programs from average ones is whether you stick with them.

### Answering from your actual numbers

Advice is only as good as what it's based on, and this is where there's a tangible difference between a chatbot and a coach.

Say you've been eating to a 1,900-calorie target for six weeks and your weight has been flat for the last three. Ask a general chatbot why, and you'll get the standard list: you might be underestimating portions, your metabolism may have adapted, try cutting 100–200 calories. This is generic advice.

A coach that can see your log can look at the actual days. Maybe your two-week average is closer to 2,100 because each Saturday and Sunday ran 700 over, and your protein has been averaging 110 g against a 150 g target. That's an answer you can act on tonight. (The [calorie guide](/blog/how-many-calories-to-lose-weight) walks through that weekend math if it sounds familiar.)

A human trainer can do this too, but only if you share your food log and they've agreed to review it. Check whether nutrition check-ins are part of what you're paying for.

### Being there at 11pm

An AI coach can answer questions at 6am before the gym and at 11pm when you're deciding whether to eat something. A quick question doesn't cost another session. That sounds minor, but small questions answered in the moment can prevent bad decisions.

### Where it can go wrong

The honest limitation: AI coaches can sound confident and still be wrong, especially on unusual questions or anything medical.

They also only have the information you log. If you forget to log half your meals, the coach will reason from half your meals. Check surprising advice before you act on it, especially if it involves your health.

## The cost, worked out

Here's what a year looks like using $70 a session as an example:

| Approach | Sessions in a year | Approximate cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trainer twice a week | 104 | $7,280 |
| Trainer once a week | 52 | $3,640 |
| 4 sessions in month 1, then one a month for 11 months | 15 | $1,050 |
| AI coach only | 0 | A small fraction of any of these |

The third row is the one we'd suggest for most beginners. You get the form coaching when it really matters, a check-in each month to catch any bad habits, and you can handle the day-to-day planning and questions yourself (or with an app).

## How to tell a useful AI coach from a chatbot

Plenty of fitness apps now add a chat window, so here are a few questions that can help you sort the useful ones from the rest:

- **Does it know your data?** It should see your meals, workouts and weight trend without you pasting them in. If you have to explain your week every time you seek guidance, it's a chatbot not a coach.
- **What can it do?** A useful coach can build a workout you can start logging, log a meal or update a goal. A chatbot would just describe how you'd do it.
- **Does it show its working?** Good answers point at your numbers ("your average is X, your target is Y"), so you can check them yourself.
- **Does it know its limits?** It should tell you when something needs a doctor or a physical therapist rather than guess.

We'd go further: an AI coach that can't see your log isn't much better than a good article. The personal part of personal training is knowing the person.

## Which one should you choose?

**If you're new to lifting** and want to learn the barbell lifts, book 3–5 sessions with a qualified trainer. Look for a recognized personal-training certification and a trainer who asks about your goals and history before loading a bar. Then plan and track the rest yourself, and book a check-in every month or two.

**If you already train consistently**, an AI coach and a good tracker will cover programming, adjustments and everyday questions. You should still book a single session when you're learning a new lift like the [Romanian deadlift](/exercises/romanian-deadlift) or when a lift has been stuck for months despite eating, sleeping and training well.

**If you struggle to show up**, pay for the appointment. Keeping you accountable is the one thing software is worst at replacing.

**If you're working around an injury or a medical condition,** start with a doctor or physical therapist, as above, then choose a trainer with relevant experience.

The best trainers charge what they do because they're good at the one thing software can't do yet, which is watch you. Spend your money on that, instead of paying someone to write you a spreadsheet.
