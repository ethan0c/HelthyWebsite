"I'm eating less, I'm training, and the scale hasn't moved in three weeks." A flat scale for that long doesn't mean your body is broken or your metabolism has shut down.

If you're eating below maintenance over time, you lose weight, though it won't all be fat. When the scale doesn't show a loss, start with two likely explanations: water is hiding it, or you're eating more than you think, usually through cooking oil, drinks, small bites and weekends. Give careful logging 3–4 weeks and compare weekly averages, not single weigh-ins.

Here are the eight reasons we see most, roughly in order of how often they turn out to be the actual answer.

## 1. Water is hiding the fat loss

Your morning weight includes fat, muscle, bone, water and whatever food is still working its way through you. Fat is the part you're trying to change, and it moves slowly. Water can swing by a kilogram (2.2 lb) or more overnight.

The usual triggers of extra weight in the morning are:

- **A salty or carb-heavy meal.** Each gram of carbohydrate you store in muscle holds roughly 3 g of water with it, so a restaurant dinner can put up to 0.5–1 kg on the scale by morning.
- **New or harder training.** Sore muscles hold extra fluid while they repair, so starting a new program, or your first leg day in a while, often shows up as a sudden jump in weight.
- **The menstrual cycle.** Many women see their weight rise in the days before a period and then drop back once it starts.
- **Poor sleep and stress.** Both are linked to fluid retention, and a rough week often affects what you see at weigh-ins.
- **Digestion.** Food in transit adds extra weight to your body that shows up on the scale in the morning.

Daily weigh-ins are like checking your bank balance while payments are still pending. A single reading won't tell you which changes have settled yet.

Here's what a real deficit can look like. This is an 85 kg (187 lb) person eating 500 calories a day below maintenance, which should take off about 0.45 kg (1 lb) of fat a week. In week 1 they started a new leg workout on Wednesday and had pizza and beer on Saturday night.

| Day | Week 1 (kg) | Week 2 (kg) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monday | 85.0 | 84.9 |
| Tuesday | 84.8 | 84.6 |
| Wednesday | 84.9 | 84.4 |
| Thursday | 85.4 | 84.5 |
| Friday | 85.2 | 84.3 |
| Saturday | 84.9 | 84.2 |
| Sunday | 85.6 | 84.4 |
| **Average** | **85.1** | **84.5** |

If you weigh only on Mondays it looks like 0.1 kg lost in a week, but if you weigh instead on Sunday of week 1, it looks like you gained 0.6 kg. The weekly averages show 0.6 kg down, which is around what the deficit predicts once week 1's water has left your system.

We suggest that you weigh every morning and judge the weekly average, rather than weighing only once a week, because a single weekly weigh-in can land on a water spike and make a good week look like a failure. If daily weighing stresses you out, weekly is fine, but do it on the same day, at the same time, after the toilet, and compare across a month, not week to week.

Don't assume "I'm gaining muscle" explains a flat scale. Beginners sometimes build muscle while dieting, but the scale alone can't tell you whether that's happening. Measure your waist at the navel once a week under the same conditions. A shrinking waist over several weeks is a useful sign of fat loss even when scale weight is flat.

## 2. You haven't given it long enough

At a sensible pace, 0.5–1% of your bodyweight a week, the fat you lose in seven days is often smaller than the daily water swing. Two weeks is barely enough to see a trend. Three to four gives you a better basis for an adjustment.

The first week of a diet also fools people. Eating fewer carbs and less food drops glycogen, water and gut contents, and the scale can fall 1–2 kg (2–4 lb). Then week 2 or 3 looks flat by comparison, even though fat loss is carrying on. That flat patch can be the initial water drop wearing off rather than a stall.

If you have periods, compare the same point in your cycle month to month rather than week to week.

## 3. You're eating more than you log

People are consistently poor at estimating what they eat, and the misses nearly always go the same way.

In a [1992 study in the *New England Journal of Medicine*](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJM199212313272701), Steven Lichtman and colleagues measured energy use over two weeks in 10 people with obesity who said they couldn't lose weight despite eating under 1,200 calories a day. They used doubly labeled water, a lab method that let them estimate actual intake from energy use and changes in body stores. Compared with those estimates, participants under-reported food intake by about 47% and over-reported physical activity by about 51%. Their metabolisms were normal for their size.

The limit matters: this was a small, selected group, long before phone tracking. It doesn't tell us how far off your log is. It does show that a sincere estimate can miss a lot.

The leaks in your diet are probably:

- **Cooking oil.** A tablespoon is about 120 calories, and a free pour into a pan is often two of those.
- **Drinks.** A large milky coffee can be 200 calories or more. A pint of beer is about 200, and a large glass of wine is in the same ballpark.
- **Bites and tastes.** The crusts off the kids' toast, a handful of nuts (about 180 calories for every 30 g), two cookies from the break room.
- **Sauces and dressings.** Mayonnaise, pesto and salad dressing are mostly fat, and they rarely make it into the log.
- **Eyeballed portions.** A "tablespoon" of peanut butter off a spoon is often closer to two.

None of these are dramatic on their own, but three of them a day is 300–500 calories, which can be your entire deficit.

## 4. Your weekends are undoing your weekdays

Here's a week of numbers for the same kind of person: maintenance around 2,500 calories, a plan of 2,000 a day, and a week that feels like a good one. Monday to Thursday they don't log the cooking oil or a couple of milky coffees. Friday is dinner out, Saturday is takeout and drinks, and Sunday is a late brunch.

| Day | Planned | Actually eaten |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monday | 2,000 | 2,250 |
| Tuesday | 2,000 | 2,250 |
| Wednesday | 2,000 | 2,250 |
| Thursday | 2,000 | 2,250 |
| Friday | 2,000 | 2,900 |
| Saturday | 2,000 | 3,300 |
| Sunday | 2,000 | 2,600 |
| **Week total** | **14,000** | **17,800** |
| **Daily average** | **2,000** | **2,543** |

The plan was a 3,500-calorie deficit for the week, roughly enough for 0.45 kg (1 lb) of weight loss. What actually happened was a small surplus. The weekday omissions added 1,000 calories; Friday through Sunday added 2,800 above the plan. It didn't take unusually large meals to close the gap.

Our view is that trying to be perfect on weekends usually fails, so plan for them instead. Eat about 1,850 on weekdays and give yourself 2,400 on Saturday and Sunday, so that the week still totals roughly 14,000. The deficit survives, and you're not white-knuckling your way through a friend's birthday.

## 5. You're eating back exercise calories

Workout calorie numbers are estimates. A [2017 Stanford study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5491979/) put seven popular wrist-worn devices on 60 volunteers and compared them with lab measurements. Heart rate was mostly decent. Calories burned were not: reported median errors ranged from about 27% to over 90%.

The limit is that these were older devices tested on a small group in a lab. Those percentages aren't an accuracy rating for your current watch, but they're a reason to check your weight trend before trusting a calorie estimate.

Two more problems stack on top. Gym machines and apps usually show the total calories burned during the session, including the calories you'd have burned sitting on the sofa anyway. And if you set your calorie target with an activity level ("moderately active, 3–5 workouts a week"), your workouts are already counted. Adding them back on top counts them twice.

For scale, a 30-minute run for a 70 kg (154 lb) person burns roughly 300 calories, depending on pace. A large muffin is often more.

For a routine already included in your calorie target, we wouldn't add workout calories back. If you've done substantially more than usual, use recovery, hunger and your weight trend to judge whether you need more food. Treating every watch calorie as extra spending money makes a deficit easy to erase.

## 6. You're moving less without noticing

Everyday movement (walking, standing, fidgeting, taking the stairs) burns more than most people's workouts, and it's the first thing that drops when you're dieting. Because you're a bit more tired, you drive instead of walking, sit instead of pacing on calls, and spend Sunday on the sofa.

Going from 9,000 steps a day to 6,000 is roughly 100–150 fewer calories a day for most people. That won't wipe out a deficit alone, but add it to a weekend and a few unlogged coffees and you're already at maintenance levels.

Set a step floor: take your current daily average and don't let it slide as the diet goes on. Helthy updates your maintenance estimate each week as your activity changes, so you can see when a quieter week calls for a different target.

## 7. Your metabolism has slowed (but probably not as much as you think)

Some slowdown is real, and part of it is simple arithmetic. A lighter body burns less. In the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, each kilogram lost lowers resting burn by 10 calories, so after losing 10 kg (22 lb) your resting burn is about 100 calories lower.

With daily activity on top, your maintenance is often about 150 lower. The deficit you started with has quietly shrunk.

On top of that, your body does adapt to dieting beyond what weight loss alone explains. The most famous example is a 2016 follow-up of 14 contestants from *The Biggest Loser*. Six years after the show, their resting metabolism was about 500 calories a day lower than expected for their size.

The limits matter more than the headline: they lost enormous amounts of weight very quickly, on a TV regimen with hours of exercise a day. That's a long way from a moderate diet.

The honest caveat: we can't give you a clean number for how much adaptation to expect, because it varies with how much you lose and how fast. Before blaming a metabolic crash, check reasons 3 and 6. The Lichtman group is a useful reminder: they believed their metabolisms were the problem, but their metabolisms measured normal.

The practical fix is the same either way. Re-estimate your maintenance at your current weight with the [TDEE calculator](/tools/tdee-calculator), and compare it with what you're eating now.

## 8. Something medical is involved

This is the least common reason, but it's a possibility. If you've logged carefully for four weeks (weighed food, weekends included) at an intake that should be a deficit and your average hasn't moved, especially if you're also unusually tired, cold or constipated, ask your doctor for a thyroid blood test. An underactive thyroid is common, easy to test for and treatable.

Some medications make weight loss harder too, including steroids such as prednisone, insulin and some other diabetes drugs, and certain antidepressants, antipsychotics and epilepsy medicines. Don't stop any of them on your own; ask whoever prescribed it whether it could be a factor. Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is another medical concern worth raising if your periods are irregular.

## What to do this week

Before you cut calories any further, fix the data. Cutting more on top of bad numbers just makes you hungrier without telling you anything.

Helthy's weight screen charts your trend over time, which is easier to read than a list of numbers. Use that or a simple weekly average while you work through these steps.

1. **Weigh yourself every morning** after using the bathroom, before food or water, and take the average at the end of the week. If daily weighing stresses you out, use the weekly approach above.
2. **Log everything for seven days**, including oil, drinks, sauces, bites and the whole weekend. Weigh food at home for this week; you'll learn what a portion looks like faster than any guide can teach you. It's tedious for a week, and it's the step people most often skip.
3. **Check your target against your current weight** with the [TDEE calculator](/tools/tdee-calculator). If you've lost a fair amount since you set it, it's probably too high.
4. **Set a step floor** at your current daily average.
5. **Stop eating back exercise calories.**

After 2–3 weeks of this, if your weekly average still hasn't moved, drop about 150–200 calories a day and give it another few weeks. Our guide to [how many calories to eat to lose weight](/blog/how-many-calories-to-lose-weight) walks through setting and adjusting the target.

If you've been dieting for less than a month and otherwise feel well, start with better logging and consistent weigh-ins; you probably need more time. If a month of careful logging still shows a flat average, review the target. If you have the symptoms described above, raise them with your doctor rather than waiting for another diet adjustment.
