Most people hear "progressive overload" and picture adding weight to the bar every week. That works for a while. Then one week the new weight doesn't move, and a lot of lifters decide they've plateaued, or that overload has stopped working for them.

It hasn't. The short answer: progressive overload means doing slightly more than you did last time, at a similar effort, over and over for months. "More" can be one extra rep, one extra set or a small jump in weight. For most lifters the most reliable way to run it is to add reps at a fixed weight until you hit the top of a rep range, then add weight and start again. And you have to write your sets down, because you can't beat last week if you don't know what last week was.

## Why doing more is the whole game

Muscle adapts to what you ask of it and not much beyond that. It works a bit like a callus: skin thickens where the shoe rubs, and only as much as it needs to. Keep the rubbing exactly the same and the callus stops growing. Lift the same 3 sets of 10 at the same weight every Monday and your body has already adapted to that; it has no reason to build more.

That's why people who train for years without tracking often look the same in year three as in year one. They're working hard. They're just repeating a workload their body has already solved.

## The ways to add "more"

There are really only three that matter:

- **More reps** with the same weight
- **More weight** for the same reps
- **More sets** of the same exercise

You'll also see "slower tempo", "shorter rests" and "better form" listed as ways to progress. We'd leave them off your list. They're hard to measure and easy to fool yourself with: a rep that feels slower this week isn't something you can compare against last month. Good technique and a consistent tempo are the baseline that makes your numbers comparable, not the progress itself. If you change your range of motion, like squatting deeper, treat it as a new starting point rather than a win.

Sets are the lever to use sparingly. Adding a set every few weeks works, but it's easy to pile on volume until every session takes two hours and you're too beaten up to add reps anywhere. For most people, reps and weight do the job.

## Double progression: the method we'd start with

Double progression is the simplest system that keeps working past the beginner stage:

1. **Pick a rep range** for the exercise, such as 8–12.
2. **Choose a weight** you can lift for the bottom of the range on every set, with 1–3 reps left in the tank.
3. **Add reps** each session, even one rep on one set, until you hit the top of the range on every set.
4. **Add the smallest weight jump** available and drop back to the bottom of the range.

Here's how it looks on a bench press, 3 sets of 8–12:

| Week | Weight | Reps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 135 lb (61 kg) | 8, 8, 8 |
| 2 | 135 lb | 10, 9, 8 |
| 3 | 135 lb | 11, 10, 10 |
| 4 | 135 lb | 12, 12, 11 |
| 5 | 135 lb | 12, 12, 12 |
| 6 | 145 lb (66 kg) | 9, 8, 8 |

A few things worth noticing. Progress isn't even: week 2 added three reps in total and week 5 added one. That's normal. The last set is usually the one that lags, and it's fine to wait for it. And in week 6 the new weight came in at 9, 8, 8 rather than exactly 8s, which tells you the jump was about right.

Those extra reps are real strength, too. Going from 8 to 12 reps at 135 lb moves your estimated one-rep max from about 170 lb to about 190 lb (77 to 86 kg). You can check your own numbers with the [one-rep max calculator](/tools/one-rep-max-calculator). It's an estimate, and it gets less accurate past about 10 reps, but it's a good way to see that a rep PR counts.

### Small exercises need rep ranges, not weight jumps

On lateral raises, curls and triceps work, the jump between dumbbells is huge in relative terms. Going from 15 lb to 20 lb dumbbells (about 7 kg to 9 kg) is a 33% increase. Nobody adds 33% to their squat in one step.

So give small exercises a wider range, like 12–20, and let reps do most of the work. You might sit on 15 lb [lateral raises](/exercises/lateral-raise) for six or eight weeks, climbing from 12 reps to 20, before the 20 lb pair is realistic for 12. If your gym has fractional plates or 2.5 lb (1 kg) increments on the cable stack, use them.

## Linear progression for beginners

If you're in your first few months of lifting, you can often skip the rep range and just add weight every session on the big lifts. Squat 3 sets of 5 at 95 lb (43 kg) on Monday, 100 lb on Wednesday, 105 lb on Friday. Upper-body lifts like the [overhead press](/exercises/overhead-press) move slower, so jumps of 2.5 lb (1 kg) or adding weight once a week are more realistic there.

It works because early strength gains are mostly your nervous system learning the movement, and that improves fast. It also can't last. Do the math: adding 5 lb three times a week is 780 lb in a year. Nobody's squat goes from 95 lb to 875 lb.

The usual pattern is that linear progression runs for somewhere between two and six months, then you start missing reps. When you miss the same weight twice, don't grind on it for a month. Drop the weight about 10%, build back up, and if you stall again at the same point, switch that lift to double progression. That's a normal transition, not a failure.

## How hard should each set be?

Progressive overload assumes each set is actually hard. This is the part most people get wrong, and in our view it's a bigger reason for stalling than any program choice.

If you finish a set of 10 knowing you could have done 15, beating it next week by a rep doesn't mean much. You were never near the edge. Aim to end most working sets with about 1–3 reps left: the bar is slowing down, your form is still intact, and one or two more would be a real fight. On big barbell lifts, stay on the conservative end of that. On machines and small dumbbell exercises you can go closer to failure safely.

Effort matters more than the exact rep range. Muscle grows across a wide range of reps, from heavy sets of 6 to light sets of 20 or more, as long as the sets are taken close to failure. Heavier sets are better for building maximal strength, which is why a lot of programs keep the main lifts in lower rep ranges and the accessories higher.

## Weight or reps: does it matter which you add?

Less than you'd think. A 2022 study had recreationally trained young adults do the same lower-body program for 8 weeks. One group progressed by adding load while staying in a set rep range; the other kept the load and added reps. Muscle growth was similar between the groups, and the differences in strength were small. It was a short study in younger lifters, and it only looked at legs, so it doesn't settle every question. But it backs up the practical point: adding reps is real progress, not a consolation prize for when you can't add weight.

## Why you have to track it

Most people can't remember what they did two sessions ago across five or six exercises. Was it 3 sets of 10 at 60 lb, or 10, 9, 8? Without the numbers you default to "about the same as usual", which is exactly the workload your body has already adapted to.

A notebook works. So does a notes app, if you're disciplined about it. The advantage of a [workout tracker](/workout-tracker) is that the record builds itself as you train. In Helthy you log weight and reps for each set, you can pull up your full history on any exercise, and new records for max weight, max reps and max volume are picked out automatically when you finish the session. That last part matters more than it sounds: a rep PR at the same weight is easy to miss on paper, and it's often the only win you'll get on a hard week.

Whatever you use, write the target down before you start the set. "Last time 135 × 11, 10, 10; today, 12 on the first set." It turns every session into one small, specific goal.

## How fast should you progress?

It depends mostly on how long you've been training consistently:

- **First 6–12 months:** weight on the main lifts every session or every week, reps on everything else.
- **Years 1–3:** reps most weeks, weight on the main lifts every few weeks. A good month on the [squat](/exercises/barbell-squat) might be 10 lb (5 kg).
- **Beyond that:** progress is measured in months. A 5–10 lb gain on the [bench press](/exercises/barbell-bench-press) over a training block is a good result.

These categories are fuzzy, and they're more about how close you are to your potential than about the calendar. Someone who has trained for five years without much structure might progress like a beginner once they start tracking.

Your diet affects all of this. It's much harder to add strength in a big calorie deficit, so if you're cutting, holding your numbers steady is a reasonable goal. If you're unsure how much to eat, the [calorie guide](/blog/how-many-calories-to-lose-weight) and [protein calculator](/tools/protein-calculator) cover it.

## When progress stalls

Everyone stalls. Call it a real plateau if a lift hasn't moved in about three weeks (reps or weight), and check these before you change program:

- **Food.** Are you eating enough, and getting enough protein? A new cut is the most common hidden cause.
- **Sleep.** A week of five-hour nights shows up in your numbers fast.
- **Effort.** Are you stopping sets with four or five reps in reserve because the last reps are unpleasant?
- **Fatigue.** After two or three months of steady pushing, a lighter week (same exercises, roughly half the sets, or 10–20% less weight) often brings the numbers back.
- **Consistency.** Missing one session in three means you're effectively training less often than your program assumes.

Only once those are sorted is it worth changing exercises or rep ranges. When you do, change one lift at a time, so you can tell what helped.

One exception: pain. A bit of soreness is normal. A sharp or joint-specific pain that shows up every time you do a lift isn't something to overload through. Swap the exercise, drop the weight, and if it still hurts after a week or two of lighter training, get it looked at by a physical therapist.

If you've been training for less than a year, run double progression on every exercise, write every set down and try to beat last time by one rep. Switch the main lifts to adding weight each session only while you're still in your first few months and it's working, and move on from that the second time you miss the same weight.
