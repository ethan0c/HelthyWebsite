If you lift the same weights for the same reps every week, your body has no reason to change. Progressive overload is the fix: gradually ask your muscles to do a bit more than they're used to. It's the single most important principle in strength training, and it's simpler than it sounds.

## What counts as "more"

You can overload a lift in several ways:

- **More weight** for the same reps
- **More reps** with the same weight
- **More sets** of the same exercise
- **Better reps**: more control, a fuller range of motion, a slower lowering phase

Adding weight is the most obvious, but it isn't the only option. For smaller exercises like lateral raises, where the jump between dumbbells can be 20% or more, adding reps is usually the better move.

## The easiest method: double progression

Double progression works for almost everyone, from beginners to experienced lifters:

1. **Pick a rep range**, for example 8–12 reps.
2. **Choose a weight** you can lift for the bottom of the range with good form.
3. **Add reps each session** until you hit the top of the range on every set.
4. **Then add weight**, drop back to the bottom of the range and start again.

Here's what that looks like on a bench press working in 3 sets of 8–12:

| Week | Weight | Reps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 135 lb | 8, 8, 8 |
| 2 | 135 lb | 10, 9, 9 |
| 3 | 135 lb | 12, 11, 10 |
| 4 | 135 lb | 12, 12, 12 |
| 5 | 145 lb | 8, 8, 8 |

Each session has one small, clear goal: beat last time by a rep or two.

## Why you have to track it

Progressive overload only works if you know what you did last time. Most people can't remember their numbers from a week ago across five or six exercises, so they end up repeating the same weights on autopilot.

Writing down every set (weight and reps) fixes this. It also gives you a record to look back on when motivation dips; seeing your squat go up 40 lb in three months is hard to argue with.

## How fast should you progress?

It depends on your training age:

- **Beginners** can often add weight to big lifts every session or every week for the first few months.
- **Intermediate lifters** might progress weekly on small exercises but only monthly on the main lifts.
- **Advanced lifters** measure progress over months, not weeks.

## When progress stalls

Everyone plateaus. If a lift hasn't moved in two to three weeks, check these before changing your program:

- **Food:** are you eating enough, especially protein? It's very hard to gain strength in a big calorie deficit.
- **Sleep:** poor sleep reliably hurts performance.
- **Effort:** are your sets close to failure, or are you stopping with five reps left in the tank?
- **Fatigue:** after months of steady progress, a lighter week (a deload) often brings the numbers back.

Only once those are sorted is it worth changing exercises, rep ranges or your split.

## The takeaway

Pick a rep range, write down every set and try to beat last time by a little. Do that consistently for months and you will get stronger. Everything else in training is detail.
