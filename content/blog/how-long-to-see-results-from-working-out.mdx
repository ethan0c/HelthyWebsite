"I've been going for three weeks and I look exactly the same." If that's you, you're probably right on schedule.

Here's the short answer. Most new lifters feel noticeably stronger within 2–4 weeks. Small changes in the mirror usually show up around 8–12 weeks, and other people tend to notice after 4–6 months of consistent training. Fat loss runs on its own clock: at a sensible 0.5–1% of your bodyweight a week, a 180 lb (82 kg) person loses roughly 1–2 lb (0.4–0.8 kg) a week.

Those timelines assume you train at least three times a week, push your sets reasonably hard and eat for your goal. They also hide a lot of variation between people, which we'll get to, because it's the part most articles on this skip.

## How fast do you get stronger?

Strength is the first result you'll see, and it comes quickly. In the first month or two, most beginners can add weight to their squat, deadlift and press every week, and often every session. The weights that felt heavy in week one start to feel ordinary by week four.

As an example, say you start squatting 95 lb (43 kg) for sets of 5 and add 5 lb (2.5 kg) a week. By week 12 you're at 150 lb (68 kg), more than 50% heavier, and plenty of beginners move faster than that early on. That pace doesn't last (nobody adds 5 lb a week for five years), but it's normal for the first few months, and it's the best motivation you'll get while the mirror catches up.

## Why beginners get stronger so fast

Most of that early strength isn't new muscle. It's your nervous system getting better at using the muscle you already have.

When you first learn a lift, your brain is clumsy at it. It recruits fewer of the available muscle fibers, fires them less in sync, and lets the opposing muscles tense up and work against you. Balance and bar path are shaky too, so some effort goes into not wobbling. Over the first weeks all of that improves, and the same muscle produces more force.

It's a lot like learning to type. In the first few weeks you get much faster, and it's not because your fingers got stronger. You just stopped hunting for the keys.

The cleanest evidence for this is a well-documented effect called cross-education: if you train only one arm or leg for several weeks, the untrained side gets somewhat stronger too. It hasn't grown, so the change has to be in the nervous system.

One honest caveat. The neat story of "nerves first, muscle later" is a simplification. Muscle starts responding from your first sessions, and researchers still disagree about how much of long-term strength gain comes from muscle size versus everything else. The practical point holds, though: fast early strength gains don't mean you've built much muscle yet, and a slowdown after a few months doesn't mean you've stopped building it.

## How long until you see muscle?

Months, not weeks.

In the first couple of weeks, your muscles can look fuller. That's mostly extra water and stored carbohydrate in freshly trained muscle, plus some swelling from the training itself. It's real in the mirror, but it's not new tissue, and it tends to level off.

Actual muscle growth is slow enough that you won't see it day to day. A common coaching rule of thumb is that a man in his first year of serious training might gain somewhere around 1–2 lb (0.5–1 kg) of muscle a month, and a woman about half that in absolute terms, slowing each year after. Treat those as ballpark figures from coaching experience rather than measured averages. Most studies that measure muscle directly last 8–12 weeks, which isn't long enough to say much about year one.

What the studies do show is how much people differ. In 2005, Monica Hubal and colleagues put 585 untrained men and women through 12 weeks of the same supervised arm-training program. Changes in the size of the trained arm muscles ranged from −2% to +59%, and gains in one-rep max ranged from nothing to about 250%. One limit: it was a single muscle group on one program, so someone who responded poorly there might do better on a different plan. But the spread is enormous, and it means your friend's 12-week transformation photo tells you very little about yours.

## How fast can you lose fat?

A sensible pace is 0.5–1% of your bodyweight a week. Faster is possible, but it tends to cost muscle, training quality and your mood.

There's decent evidence for going slower if you care about muscle. In a 2011 Norwegian study led by Ina Garthe, 24 elite athletes who were strength training lost weight at either about 0.7% or 1.4% of their bodyweight per week. The slower group gained a little lean mass and improved more on strength tests, while the faster group roughly held steady. Elite athletes aren't typical, and 24 people is a small study, but the direction matches what coaches see: slower cuts keep more of what you've built.

Here's what those rates look like over 12 weeks:

| Week | 200 lb (91 kg) at 0.75% a week | 160 lb (73 kg) at 0.5% a week |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 200 lb | 160 lb |
| 4 | 194 lb | 157 lb |
| 8 | 188 lb | 154 lb |
| 12 | 183 lb (83 kg) | 151 lb (68 kg) |

(The weekly loss shrinks slightly as you get lighter, which is why the numbers aren't perfectly even.)

Two things make the scale confusing early on. The first week of a diet often drops a few pounds at once, mostly water and stored carbohydrate, and that pace won't continue. And if you've just started lifting, your muscles hold extra water too, which can hide fat loss for the first few weeks. If you want the full picture on setting that pace, see [how many calories to eat to lose weight](/blog/how-many-calories-to-lose-weight).

When you see it depends on where you start. Someone with a lot to lose sees the scale move sooner. A leaner person losing the last 10 lb sees less on the scale but more in the mirror, because each pound comes off a smaller frame.

## A realistic timeline for a beginner

Rough expectations for someone new to lifting who trains three or four times a week and eats for their goal:

| Time | Strength | Muscle | Fat loss (at 0.5–1% a week) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2–4 weeks | Clearly stronger; weights going up most sessions | Muscles look a bit fuller, mostly water | 2–4% of bodyweight, partly water |
| 8–12 weeks | Main lifts well up from week 1; progress starting to slow | Small changes you can see in photos | 4–12% of bodyweight |
| 6 months | Progress now weekly or fortnightly on main lifts | Visible to other people; clothes fit differently | 12–23% if you dieted the whole time (most people take breaks) |
| 1 year | Progress measured in months | A clear change from day one | Depends on how many diet phases you ran |

Individual variation is large enough that you could land a column to the left or right of this and still be completely normal.

## What to measure (and how often)

The mirror is a bad day-to-day measuring tool. You see yourself every day, so slow changes are invisible. Pick at least two of these instead:

- **Weight trend.** Weigh yourself most mornings, after the toilet and before eating, and look at the weekly average. We'd pick this over a single weekly weigh-in, because daily weight can swing 2–4 lb (1–2 kg) on water and salt alone, and one weekly reading can easily land on a high day and ruin your week for no reason.
- **Tape measurements.** Waist at your belly button is the most useful one. Add hips, chest, upper arm and thigh if you like. Measure every two weeks, same time of day, tape snug but not tight.
- **Photos.** Front, side and back, every 2–4 weeks, in the same spot, the same light and the same clothes. They're the best way to see muscle gain that the scale can't show.
- **Your lifts.** Log every set. For beginners, a rising squat and bench are the most reliable sign things are working, especially in the first two months before the mirror catches up.

One thing we'd skip: bathroom scales that estimate body fat. They send a small current through your body and infer fat from how easily it passes, and that shifts with how hydrated you are. A reading can jump a couple of percent overnight for reasons that have nothing to do with fat, which makes them poor for tracking change.

Keeping all this in one place helps more than people expect, since comparing week 1 with week 12 is the whole point. ## What to do when progress seems slow

Work down this list in order. Most stalls get solved in the first four.

1. **Check the calendar.** If it's been less than 8 weeks, you probably aren't slow. You're early.
2. **Count your sessions.** Look at the last four weeks and count the workouts you actually did, not the ones you planned. Two a week instead of four is the most common cause of slow results, and the easiest to fix.
3. **Check whether the weights are going up.** If you're lifting the same loads for the same reps as a month ago, your body has no reason to change. Our guide to [progressive overload](/blog/progressive-overload) covers how to fix that.
4. **Check your effort.** Most sets should end with only 1–3 reps left in the tank. If you're stopping with five or six to spare, the weight is too light.
5. **Check your food.** For muscle, the usual gap is protein; see [how much protein you need](/blog/how-much-protein-to-build-muscle). For fat loss, it's usually untracked calories from oils, drinks and weekends. [Why am I not losing weight?](/blog/why-am-i-not-losing-weight) goes through the common culprits.
6. **Check your sleep.** Regularly getting under 6 hours makes training harder and hunger worse.
7. **Only then change the program.** Swapping programs every few weeks resets the progress you were about to make.

If you've carefully tracked your intake for four weeks or more, your weight average hasn't moved, and you're also unusually tired or cold all the time, ask your doctor for a thyroid blood test. It's one of the less likely explanations for a stall, but it's easy to test for and treatable.

If you're a beginner, give any sensible plan 12 weeks of consistent effort before you judge it, and compare month to month rather than week to week. Strength will tell you it's working first. The mirror will catch up later.
